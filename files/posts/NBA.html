Building a Championship Team in Today’s NBA

I’m not going to say I called it, but I did.

Minutes before tipoff of Game 1 of the NBA Finals, I went on the record: “The Knicks are going to win this series.” It wasn’t exactly a scorching-hot take, but the more I watched New York throughout its Eastern Conference playoff run, the more convinced I became that this team checked every box of a modern NBA champion. The Spurs, on the other hand, simply weren’t there yet.

So what’s the secret sauce to winning in today’s NBA?

Let’s break down the traits that made the Knicks championship-worthy, compare them to the title winners of the past decade, and see why their victory shouldn’t have come as much of a surprise.

Modern NBA championships are built on five core ingredients: a prior deep playoff run, continuity, elite star power, versatile 3-and-D wings (including switchable defenders and floor-spacing bigs), and a veteran bench, led by a reliable backup point guard, that minimizes mistakes.

These are the five boxes I’ll use to evaluate each team. Before diving in, here’s a closer look at the details for each one.

Playoff Run Experience: Nine of the 11 teams here had a deep (conference finals appearance or longer) run in the playoffs in the years leading up to their championship run with their core group that ultimately won the championship.

Outlier: 2019 Raptors. Two conference semifinal runs in 2017 and 2018. Those teams had Ibaka, Powell, Lowry, FVV, and Siakam, but lacked Kawhi and Danny Green, whom they acquired in the DeRozan trade (absolutely bananas trade btw: Kawhi and Danny for DeRozan, who is two years older than Kawhi, Jakob Poeltl, and a protected 2019 first-round draft pick...???). They later traded for Marc Gasol.

Outlier: 2025 Thunder. Lost in the conference semifinals in 2024. The turnaround they then had in 2025 was remarkable but not surprising. This miraculously quick improvement could very easily be one that the 2027 Spurs will look to replicate.

Continuity: Ten of the 11 teams here had a core group of players continuously on the team for a minimum of two full seasons. Core is a little subjective here; you either had your 2-3 stars (like the 2016 Cavs and 2024 Celtics) or you had several (5+) edge pieces (like the 2019 Raptors) that gave the team its overall shape and guidance in the years leading up to its championship run.

Outlier: 2020 Lakers. When you have a fully healthy roster that includes LeBron James and Anthony Davis, you’re going to win.

Elite Stars: Every one of these teams had at least one Hall of Fame–level player on its roster and one or more additional All-Star(s) and/or All-NBA player(s).

3-and-D Wings: These are players that are usually 6'6" or taller; they can catch and shoot 3-pointers at a high efficiency; they don’t need the ball to score; and they can defend 1-5 at the other end. Each team here has a plethora of these types of players. You simply cannot have enough switchable and versatile wings on your roster. Secondary here are the evolving floor-spacing bigs that can switch onto smaller guards and wings and can still shoot threes. They are not as good perimeter defenders as a typical 3-and-D wing, but they can space the floor just as well, pass, and protect the inside better than most.

Low-Mistake Veterans: Each of these champions brought reliable players off the bench that formed their core ~eight-man rotation. These players were most often veterans of the league (30+ years of age) or younger guys who the “public” never had high expectations of. See, the key to being a championship-level rotational player is you can’t have an attitude or a big ego. You need to come in and play your role: don’t turn the ball over, don’t make mistakes, take what the defense gives you, make the easy shots, and don’t try to be the hero. Doing all of these things also helps your team to not blow leads or give up big runs while your stars are on the bench. These players have learned to play off the bench perfectly; yes, they still want to make an impact and win the game, but they know exactly the lines not to cross.

On paper, the Knicks had it all. And as I thought about writing this piece as I was watching the Finals, the stars started to align. This time they had everything they needed to win. They had the playoff experience from the year before, learning what to do and not to do to close out games. They had more than enough continuity. They put together their starting lineup in 2024 after acquiring Bridges and KAT in trades. Brunson, Bridges, and Hart all played at Villanova together and won an NCAA championship in 2016. Brunson proved himself to be the clutchest player in the league and a certified MVP candidate for next year. Their versatility was evident throughout the playoffs. OG, KAT, Bridges, and Hart could switch continuously and lock down some of the best offensive players in the league on their title run. They rounded out their rotation with a reliable and older bench of Shamet, Alvarado, Robinson, Clarkson, and McBride. Shamet and Alvarado clearly won Mike Brown’s trust as they both played meaningful fourth-quarter minutes in multiple games throughout the Finals.

On the other hand, the Spurs lacked most notably in the experience column (although they have now checked this box for next year) and their bench reliability down the stretch. Their weaknesses showed in clutch* moments: shooting 13-for-34 (38.2%) from the field, 3-for-10 (30%) from three, and 10-for-16 (62.5%) from the line. It’s going to be tough winning Finals games when the average age of your sixth through eighth men (Harper, Johnson, Bryant) off the bench is 22. Whether coaching or player decision making, they simply couldn’t close out games: the Spurs blew double-digit leads in four of the five games, had the lead for over 70% of the total series, and choked away a franchise-record 29-point lead in the largest collapse in NBA Finals history in Game 4. On the bright side, this Finals series is the perfect learning experience for them to take and come back next year to get over the hump.

*When the score is within five points in the last five minutes of the fourth quarter or overtime.

A Side Rant on Another Spurs Anomaly

In the 21st century, a majority of top-five picks haven’t even won a single playoff series for the team that drafted them (or traded for them on draft night), and a vanishingly small percentage have won a championship.

Only three out of 26 Finals MVP trophies this century have gone to players who landed on a team via its own top-five pick: Tim Duncan (2x), Dwyane Wade.

More broadly, only five players who landed on a team with its own top-five pick have even been rotation players for championship teams this century: Duncan (4x), David Robinson, Wade (3x), Tristan Thompson, and Chet. But this San Antonio group could add three more by itself if it wins a title and most likely add a name to the Finals MVP list as well. The Spurs landed Wemby (at No. 1), Castle (at No. 4) and Harper (at No. 2) in consecutive drafts from 2023 to 2025.

Between Dwyane Wade in 2003 and Chet in 2022, Tristan Thompson — a role player who averaged 6.7 playoff points for the 2016 Cavaliers — was the only player drafted in the top five with a team’s own pick who helped that franchise win a title. This all means that an increasing number of NBA championship rosters are built from a mix of free agency, trades, and the draft, though not often top picks within the draft. As we just reviewed, many of the above teams acquired role players from other teams via free agency and trades the year before or the year of their championship run. The 2026 Spurs would have been a true outlier in this sense as well; however, they still have the best chance — and the last chance — to prove the power of the NBA lottery system by keeping their three recent top-five picks together. (The NBA’s new lottery rules would forbid a team from picking in the top five in three consecutive drafts.)

There have been 23 occasions in the lottery era in which a team picked in the top five in three consecutive drafts. More than half of those cores never won a single playoff series together and only three of those cores ever made a Finals trip: the Thunder with Durant, Westbrook, and Harden; the Cavs with Irving, Thompson, Dion Waiters, Anthony Bennett and Andrew Wiggins (whom they immediately traded for Kevin Love); and the Spurs with Wemby, Castle, and Harper. The first lost to the Heat Big 3, and the second needed LeBron to return in free agency to achieve a winning record, let alone reach the Finals. The 2026 Spurs cap off a remarkable season and an amazing achievement by reaching the Finals this year and by all accounts of NBA history are already an outlier in their own right. It will simply be even more impressive, and they will stand alone in league record books, if they can win a ring with their current makeup.

The Perfect NBA Team

I’ve spent a lot of time trying to slap labels onto different players and sorting them into specific groups or archetypes, when in reality, the positionless basketball player is both the epitome of the modern NBA and the future. To be as specific as I can, these are the three common denominators that stand out to me the most when building an NBA roster in the 2020s.

A Backup Point Guard

Each champion on this list had depth, and they had depth where it matters. Just like in March Madness, what often wins are low-mistake veterans, and you want those players to be handling the ball a lot, i.e., your point guards. In playoff tournaments these guys become 100x more important because championships are decided by timely turnovers, stagnant offensive stretches, and poor defensive performances. In the NBA, having high-level backup point guards gives a team the ability to stabilize the offense, limit costly mistakes, and prevent opponents from going on runs. Senior guards win in the NCAA, and veteran guards win in the NBA.

Look at the likes of Alvarado (I never thought I would actually be saying this about him), Caruso, Pritchard, Bruce Brown, Gary Payton II, Connaughton, FVV, Livingston, Delly... these are all high quality players who could start on other non-contender NBA teams, and they are crucial in allowing their starting star guards to rest. They can be trusted playing key minutes in pivotal moments and ultimately will decide the final series. You can also quickly find the weak link when looking at the runner-up in these NBA Finals. These teams had a stacked 1-5, but you need a reliable bench and a trustworthy backup PG to make you a champion. 2019 FVV had one of the greatest backup PG Finals performances maybe ever — he ended up playing the 4th most minutes on the team right behind Lowry and shot a near 50-40-90. 2020 Goran Dragić got injured in Game 1 for the Heat, paving the way for Rondo to lead the Lakers’ second unit to a title, and 2023 Bruce Brown averaged the 4th most PPG on that Nuggets team. The list goes on.

Multiple Versatile Wings

The new positionless basketball player has simply been classified as a wing. There’s no such thing anymore as a shooting guard, or a small forward, or a power forward; you have to be able to do it all. LeBron revolutionized this narrative, followed by those like Kawhi and Tatum. These are the Hall of Fame–level examples, but the positionless wing breaks down in its simplest form to long and tall athletic players who can score from anywhere and can defend 1-5. More than any other roster archetype, these are the players who consistently prove to be essential pieces on championship teams. They provide the versatility, defensive flexibility, and reliable shooting needed to survive long playoff series. You can plug and play them very easily into different rosters because they don’t need to handle the ball to score and impact the game at the highest level. Each one of the past champions has had a plethora of these types of players, and you simply cannot have too many. Several names repeatedly appear on the above list and more impressively across multiple franchises. This is the short list of players that any team would be lucky enough to have and are bona fide championship cornerstones.

3 Rings, 3 Teams:
Danny Green — The pinnacle of elite 3-and-D.

3 Rings, 2 Teams:
JaVale — I don’t know if there’s much more here other than a rim runner and rebounder with no ego who can fit well into any contender.

2 Rings, 2 Teams:
Jrue & Rondo — The ultimate 3-and-D pass-first point guards.
KCP & Caruso — Why, why, why did the Lakers ever give them up.
JR — Eerily identical in size to Danny Green, although a slightly more volatile version of him.

1 Ring, 1 Team:
OG Anunoby — 6'7" with a 7'2" wingspan. A knockdown shooter and lockdown defender. High basketball IQ, doesn’t make mistakes or turn the ball over, clutch, reliable, and trustworthy down the stretch. And what separates him from a lot of these other 3-and-D guys is his ability to put up high scoring games. OG already has 15 career 30-point games (more than Danny Green, KCP and Caruso combined), now 9 away from tying JR’s record, and he’s just hitting his prime.

A Stretch 5

It’s no surprise that you need 3-point shooting to win NBA titles in the modern era. More importantly, you need all 5 of your players on the court to be a threat from 3, meaning you can space your offense and spread the defense at the same time. When small-ball was overtaking the league in the mid-2010s, we had players like 6'5" PJ Tucker and 6'6" Draymond Green playing “Center.” This was the first glimpse into what a stretch 5 could do for a team. Imagine if they were both 6'10" or taller? You could play a 5-out, completely stretch the floor and the defense now has to seriously cover 5 perimeter shooters. The Warriors obviously mastered this. Others followed suit. The 2016 Cavs team was bigger and therefore had to make a lot of decisions on whether to match the Warriors’ small-ball with another wing or keep their non-shooting center, Tristan Thompson, in. Future teams saw this and made the change so that they didn’t have to deal with this decision. They could get the best of both worlds with a floor-spacing big who could shoot, i.e. AD, Marc Gasol, Brook Lopez, Jokic, Horford, Porzingis, Chet, and KAT. You still need size in today’s NBA, it just exponentially helps when that size can shoot. Those that can’t can still be seen playing reliable minutes as a backup (Serge Ibaka, Dwight Howard, Isaiah Hartenstein, or Mitchell Robinson), but I have a feeling that these players will continue to fade out unless they can adapt, and quickly, to hucking threes.

We are winning 8 rings in a row.
