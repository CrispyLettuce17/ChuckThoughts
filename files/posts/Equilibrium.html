The March to Equilibrium

Storm in a Teacup

I’ve been wanting to write about something a little more personal. While reading Storm in a Teacup: The Physics of Everyday Life by Helen Czerski, I came across a few paragraphs that really stuck with me. I’ve paraphrased them below.

The physical world is always ticking toward equilibrium. This is a fundamental physical law known as the Second Law of Thermodynamics. But there’s nothing in the rulebook to say how quickly it has to get there. Every injection of energy moves things away from equilibrium, and the shift back has to start all over again. Life itself can exist because it exploits this system, using it to move energy around by controlling the speed of flow toward equilibrium.

Considering how common life is on this planet, it’s surprising that no one can come up with a single definition of what it is. We know it when we see it, but the living world can usually provide an exception to any simple rule. One definition has to do with maintaining a non-equilibrium situation and using that situation to build complex molecular factories that can reproduce themselves and evolve. Life is something that can control the speed at which energy flows through its system, manipulating the stream to maintain itself. Nothing that is in equilibrium can be alive. And this means that the concept of disequilibrium is fundamental to two of the great mysteries of our time: How did life start? And is there life anywhere else in the universe?

Scientists currently think that life may have started in deep-sea hydrothermal vents 3.7 billion years ago. Inside the vents was warm, alkaline water. Outside was cooler, slightly acidic ocean water. As they mixed at the surface of the vent, equilibrium was reached. Early life may have started by standing in the middle of this path to equilibrium; the flow toward it was diverted to build the first biological molecules. That first diversion may have then evolved into a cell membrane, the wall around each cell that separates inside, where there is life, from outside, where there is not. The first cell was successful because it could hold back equilibrium, and that was the gateway to the beautiful complexity of our living world. The same is probably true for other worlds. The Keck Observatory and its telescopes may soon be able to detect atmospheres that are not in equilibrium: too much oxygen to be sustainable, too much methane… These could betray the existence of life down on the planet. We may never know for certain, but that may be the closest we ever come to knowing that there are other organisms out there: the evidence of something controlling the speed of the march to equilibrium as it builds living complexities that we may never see.

(Side note: Brilliant Green: The Surprising History and Science of Plant Intelligence by Stefano Mancuso and Alessandra Viola is another fun book that wrestles with a similar problem: finding a single definition of what makes something intelligent.)

The third paragraph is a slight diversion from where I wanted to go, but I enjoy the beginning-of-life story. What I really found thought-provoking was the idea of this compulsive movement toward equilibrium. The Second Law says that an isolated system will always move from an unstable, unequal state toward a state of perfect, uniform balance — that nature fundamentally despises differences and will spend all its time moving things around until everything is completely even.

It sounds a lot like those who struggle with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD): people who spend all of their time moving the things around them until everything is completely even — or right, in their minds. I relate to this on a personal level. There is something oddly yet deeply satisfying about having the things around me straight, lined up, and symmetrical.

Here’s a good example. I had never owned a shower caddy before. They always seemed like too much work — you’ll see why. My girlfriend bought one when we moved into our apartment together, and it now hangs off our showerhead. If it hangs unevenly, I have to move the shampoo, conditioner, and soap bottles around so the weight is evenly distributed and the caddy hangs parallel to the ground. When an older bottle runs low, its weight drops, and it throws off the equilibrium of the whole caddy. So I have to start the reshuffling process all over again. How much water and time have I wasted standing in the shower reorganizing the shower caddy?!

Now, I don’t actually have OCD. I mean, I’ve never been tested for it. But these tics have been a part of my life for as long as I can remember. And I found Czerski’s description of nature’s inherent movement toward equilibrium fascinating. Is this why my brain has a compulsive need to keep certain things in equilibrium? Is this my brain’s way of telling me I’m alive? The parallel between nature’s desire for an evened-out world and my own is odd yet deeply satisfying. It’s like nature validating my own thoughts and desires.

Life constantly delivers jolts of energy into my orderly world, moving my state — emotional and physical — away from equilibrium. I then spend however much time is needed to move my world back into equilibrium before I can move on to the next task. Part of me truly enjoys that time spent moving toward equilibrium — the cleaning, organizing, and tidying — more than the finished product. Okay, probably all of me enjoys that part more. It’s something I could do for hours, every day, and the joy never seems to fade.

It’s a constant battle to keep my life in order. Equilibrium never lasts long, and maybe that’s a good thing, because biologically speaking, reaching perfect equilibrium means death. Constant equilibrium is boring anyway.

When I worked at Brown, I could work from home on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays. My Sundays were spent at the New England Aquarium, which meant Mondays became my new Sundays. I would wake up around 6:30, work out until 7, and then spend 7 to 9 doing a full, deep clean of our apartment. I’d put in my AirPods and listen to an audiobook. Start the laundry; make the bed; make the couch; unload the dishwasher; wash the counters; flip the laundry; vacuum; clean the bathroom; do some general tidying; fold the laundry; organize the closet. Genuinely, this morning routine was sometimes my favorite part of the week. And if we were messy enough on Tuesday, I got to repeat the process Wednesday morning!

Since I started my job at Milton, I already miss those work-from-home mornings. Without them, or my old commute, I don’t have nearly as much time for audiobooks either. Not that I miss the commute.

Anyway, after reading Storm in a Teacup, I do feel that nature has validated my needs in a way. It’s as if my body is telling my mind that what it’s feeling is normal — in fact, an inherent part of life. My mind’s compulsive need to reach equilibrium is matched by my body’s biological drive to do the same. It’s like finding someone who shares your weird hobby, who matches your freak. And they were right under your nose the whole time — literally.
