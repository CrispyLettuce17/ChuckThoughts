Make America Green Again

Renewable Energy for National Security

Last week, President Donald Trump came out and said that we need to start investing in renewable and clean energy systems in order to best protect our national security interests and to prioritize the America First agenda, specifically when it comes to our competition with China for global hegemony.

No, he did not actually put out this statement, unfortunately. But it is fun to romanticize if he did. How quickly would the MAGA fanbase jump on board? How about if Trump also mentioned that a more stable and diverse energy base would improve our economy, military, and our global superiority? Our country would be richer, safer, more powerful, and cleaner. Who would disagree besides big oil?

We need the 48th President of the U.S., from either party, to run on this bipartisan platform, because we actually need renewables to defend our nation — socially, economically, militarily, and environmentally — moving forward.

Artificial Intelligence & the Electron Gap

China’s campaign to expand its power grid dates back to the 1970s. CCP leaders worried that electricity shortages would hamper the nation’s development and steered state-owned companies to build hundreds of coal-fired power plants. In the early 2000s, they then bet on renewables, bankrolling enormous hydroelectric projects, solar fields, and wind farms.

More recently, China’s power push stems from a plan unveiled by the government in 2021, known as “East Data, West Computing.” The plan calls for harnessing abundant power resources in the nation’s west to meet AI-driven demand from the populous east. China also aims to connect hundreds of data centers to build a shared nationwide compute pool — what some describe as a “national cloud” — by 2028, using the world’s biggest network of ultrahigh-voltage transmission lines, in which it has invested more than $50 billion since 2021.

China now has the biggest power grid the world has ever seen. Between 2010 and 2024, its power production increased by more than the rest of the world combined. It has double the power-generation capacity of the U.S.. It has 34 nuclear reactors under construction and nearly 200 others planned or proposed. In Tibet, China is building the world’s largest hydropower project, which could produce three times the power of its Three Gorges Dam, already the world’s largest power station by installed capacity.

Because of this production, Chinese data centers can now secure power for as little as 3 cents a kilowatt-hour; whereas in the U.S., we typically pay 7 to 9 cents a kilowatt-hour.

And this power gap is just the beginning. China will spend some $560 billion on grid projects through 2030, up 45% from the previous five years. By 2030, China will have about 400 gigawatts of spare capacity, about three times the world’s expected data-center power demand at that time. TL;DR: Energy availability will not be a constraint for China.

Comparatively, in the next three years, U.S. data centers could face an electricity shortfall of 44 gigawatts, posing a serious challenge for our AI ambitions. Eighteen states (like Texas, Virginia, Arizona, and Nevada) have over half (116 gigawatts) of their planned solar and storage capacity at risk of being blocked due to heightened scrutiny under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA).

Furthermore, because our grid cannot expand fast enough to keep up with energy demands, local communities are taking matters into their own hands. Nearly 70 U.S. jurisdictions and multiple states (including Georgia, Michigan, Maine, and New York) have introduced temporary bans, moratoriums, or strict energy-use limits on new data centers to protect their local water and power grids.

We have been focused on turning the U.S. into an export-dominant country rather than an import-reliant one, especially on imports from China. At the same time, China has once again demonstrated that it is ahead of the curve. It already dominates, and will continue dominating, the export of clean and renewable energy technologies. It does, and will, produce these systems more cheaply and quickly than we do, and than we will.

Between 2005 and 2022, U.S. power generation hovered steadily between roughly 4,000 and 4,100 terawatt-hours (TWh) per year. Steady economic growth was completely offset by massive advancements in energy efficiency and technology. This stagnation is going to end, and end dramatically.

AI data centers currently hover around 4% of U.S. power generation. By 2030 they will use 9% to 17% of total U.S. electricity generation, capturing nearly half of all net U.S. electricity growth over the next four years.

By 2030, the U.S. will need to consume over 5,000 TWh; that is a 25% increase over the next 4 years. We’re going 0 to 100, and we better be ready. And what happens if we don’t? If actual domestic power production cannot hit the 5,000+ TWh mark by 2030, tech companies will be forced to delay AI rollouts, and local jurisdictions will continue blocking new data centers because the physical production capacity simply does not exist on the grid. Maybe these tech companies will go overseas and continue their production there, taking the jobs and the money with them. Can you see that this is the same cycle we are trying to break right now? TL;DR: Energy availability will most certainly be a constraint for us.

Most simply, our current administration needs to drop the act. Trump has already agreed that we need to match China when it comes to energy production within the AI race. Why can’t we all just admit that this needs to be done with new clean energy? Instead, we’re going to continue to halt new clean-energy construction until 2029, by which point it will be too late.

Better yet, we have now agreed to three deals totaling $2.5 billion with multiple energy developers to abandon their already approved offshore wind projects and redirect that capital toward fossil fuel investments instead. 🙃

Energy Independence & the New Three

Let’s do another hypothetical. What happens when other countries are in need of a cheaper energy source? Let’s say their current fuel source is blocked by, I don’t know, say a war in the Middle East?

Our war in Iran has sent oil-starved countries scrambling for an alternate fuel source. Not so coincidentally, many are turning to China, now the world leader in renewables. As a result, Chinese exports of solar technology, batteries, and electric vehicles all reached record highs in March, a sign that the historic oil supply shock is accelerating the adoption of clean energy around the world. Maybe that’s one positive out of this war.

The “new three” (solar, batteries, and EVs) rose 70% year over year (YOY) in March. The new three contribute significantly to China’s GDP in place of clothing, home appliances, and furniture exports that previously drove growth.

Oil price volatility has surged as the conflict has expanded throughout the Middle East and negotiations to end the war have stalled again and again. Uncertainty over when the Strait of Hormuz will reopen has spurred deeper regional and global anxieties about energy security. The U.S. would do well to take notice of the benefits of having less reliance on foreign imports for our energy source. Additionally, even the demand for oil long term is not so certain, as EVs have soared in popularity.

Of course, China has already known all of this for quite some time. The massive state investments in green energy industries that we discussed earlier have bolstered its energy self-sufficiency, reducing its exposure to the recent oil shortage. Its dominance in renewable technology has also granted the country more geopolitical and economic influence as it exports its technology. Fifty countries set new records in March for Chinese solar imports, with the most significant growth coming from emerging markets in Asia and Africa that have been hit hardest by the energy crisis. Pakistan has been spared some of the impact from the war, since it began drastically importing cheap Chinese solar panels a few years ago. Using solar energy rather than costly oil imports is estimated to save the country billions of dollars each year. China’s battery exports reached $10 billion in March, with particularly high growth rates in the EU, Australia, and India. China’s EV exports reached a record $9.2 billion, up 49% YOY, in May, up from the previous record in April at $9.1 billion. It won’t be long until BYD breaks into Canadian and American markets.

Thanks to the shale revolution, we still hold a strong and distinct advantage in fossil fuel and nuclear self-sufficiency, as a net exporter of petroleum and liquefied natural gas (LNG) and as the world’s leading oil and gas producer. Nevertheless, the past few months have shed a glaring light on our need to diversify our energy sources and on our missing out on the massive economic boost from new three exports.

As the 21st century rolls on, climate change will continue to have an unignorable impact on our world’s day-to-day life; consequently, we will see an increase in conflicts fought over scarce resources.

In 2021, President Joe Biden signed an Executive Order (EO) instructing the federal government to use electricity that is 100% free of carbon pollution by 2035. Trump revoked this EO on his first day in office; nevertheless, the signal is that inevitably our national government, including the Department of Defense (DoD), will be powered by clean energy. Past projects have included the Navy’s Great Green Fleet; a push for biofuel for Air Force jets; a contract with General Motors Defense to implement ground-based electric vehicles; an increase in the installation of microgrids; the implementation of solar-powered blankets and backpacks; and the unveiling of a floating solar array at Fort Bragg, North Carolina. It will provide backup power to the nearby training facility during blackouts, as in the case of possible climate-change-related hurricanes in the area. The cooling effect of the water allows the solar farm to generate much more electricity than traditional solar panels. All of these examples are how the U.S. military is using clean energy to enhance national security, achieve greater fuel independence, and build operational resilience against climate-related disruptions.

Lastly, this summer’s scorching temperatures have shed light on the vulnerability of our current electric grid. Electricity shortages across the country have utility executives and power grid operators warning that our system is increasingly unstable and vulnerable to blackouts. In July, a record heat wave brought our power grid in 13 states and D.C. to the brink, triggering a federal emergency order forcing large-load users (like data centers) to switch to backup generators, and directing grid operators to maximize power generation, which even included running plants past normal pollution limits to keep up with surging air-conditioning demand.

More renewable power would ease these strains. Yet as we’ve already seen, we’re heading in the opposite direction. Skyrocketing demand from data centers and Americans’ increasingly electrified daily lives have added to consumers’ rising electricity bills, and another war in the Middle East sent oil prices past $110 per barrel and gas prices over $4.50 in March, further straining the American citizen’s wallet.

I hope the above reasons will spur voters both in this upcoming midterm election and in 2028 to push for renewables as a way to stay competitive in the AI race: to bolster production of clean energy systems, batteries, and EVs, and to become less dependent on foreign energy so we can be more flexible and adaptable to future wars and climate crises.

Here’s a quote from Obama’s comprehensive strategic framework, “Blueprint for a Secure Energy Future,” developed in 2011, that I will leave as my conclusion:

“And beyond our efforts to reduce our dependence on oil, we must focus on expanding cleaner sources of electricity, including renewables like wind and solar, as well as clean coal, natural gas, and nuclear power — keeping America on the cutting edge of clean energy technology so that we can build a 21st century clean energy economy and win the future.”
