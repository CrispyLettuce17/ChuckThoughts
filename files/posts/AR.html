The American Revolution: A History

CHRONOLOGY

1763
February 10The French and Indian War ends with the Peace of Paris. France cedes Canada and all lands east of the Mississippi River to Great Britain, essentially removing French power from mainland North America. Britain emerges victorious yet severely indebted, which will later lead the Crown to find new sources of revenue.
May–NovemberChief Pontiac leads an Indian rebellion in the Ohio Valley. The Peace of Paris displaces Native American tribes and prompts them to unite against encroaching British settlers, unfair trade practices, and hostile military policies. The uprising ultimately led the British to recognize Indigenous sovereignty and to issue the aforementioned Proclamation.
October 7The Royal Proclamation of 1763 bans all westward migration in the colonies. Issued by King George III, it was designed to appease Native Americans after the war to avoid costly border conflicts; settlers deeply resented the restriction and many defied the decree entirely, both contributing significantly to broader colonial frustrations with the Crown.
1764
April 5 and 19Parliament passes the Sugar and Currency Acts to help pay off massive British war debts from the Seven Years’ War. The Sugar Act increased duties on imported foreign goods while the Currency Act banned colonies from issuing paper money, forcing debts to be paid in gold or silver. The sudden enforcement of these twin laws devastated colonial trade networks and sparked early, widespread outcry across the colonies.
1765
March 22Parliament passes the Stamp Act, the first direct tax on the American colonies. The response: “No taxation without representation!”
May 15Parliament passes the Quartering Act of 1765, requiring colonial assemblies to fund and provide housing, food, and supplies for British troops stationed in North America.
October 7The Stamp Act Congress convenes. Twenty-seven delegates from nine of the 13 colonies meet to draft a unified protest against the Stamp Act.
1766
March 18Parliament repeals the Stamp Act and passes the Declaratory Act. The latter asserts that the American colonies are subordinate to the Crown and declares that Parliament has the absolute power to make laws binding the colonies “in all cases whatsoever.”
1767
June 29Parliament passes the Townshend Acts, which impose duties on essential imported goods like glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea, establish strict customs enforcement, and suspend the New York assembly. The acts are designed to collect revenue to pay the salaries of colonial governors and judges, ensuring they remain loyal to the Crown rather than being influenced by colonial assemblies.
November 5John Dickinson’s Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania begins publication. The letters are written to protest the Townshend Acts, arguing that Parliament could regulate trade but had no constitutional right to tax the American colonies for revenue without their representation in Parliament.
1768
February 11Samuel Adams composes the Massachusetts “circular letter.” Also in protest of the Townshend Acts, the letter declares taxation without representation unconstitutional and calls for unified colonial resistance against the empire.
June 8British troops are sent to Boston in direct response to colonial opposition to imperial policies and tax laws. (1,000 soldiers in 1768; 4,000 soldiers by 1774.).
1770
March 5Boston Massacre. British soldiers fire into an angry mob of American colonists on King Street in Boston, killing five people and injuring six others.
April 12The Townshend duties are repealed, except for the duty on tea. British Parliament, led by Prime Minister Lord North, voted to repeal the Townshend duties because the colonial non-importation agreements (colonists collectively pledging to stop importing and consuming British goods until the restrictive legislation was repealed) were causing severe economic damage to British merchants; the duty on tea remained to continue to bankroll colonial governors and judges and to hold the little remaining power the Crown had over the colonists.
1772
June 9The British ship Gaspée is burned off the coast of Warwick, Rhode Island, an act of overt colonial rebellion that marked the first time American colonists shed British military blood. Merchant John Brown organized a group of prominent citizens (later calling themselves the Sons of Liberty) to board the ship, force the British captain and crew to surrender, and set the ship aflame.
November 2Bostonians publish The Votes and Proceedings, enumerating British violations of American rights. The publication was a massive political milestone that transformed sporadic, localized protests into a unified, intercolonial movement and was the first official, comprehensive manifesto that systematically listed every colonial grievance against the British Empire.
1773
January 6Massachusetts governor Thomas Hutchinson argues the supremacy of Parliament before the General Court. He argues there is no middle ground between absolute parliamentary supremacy and total colonial independence. He insists that Parliament’s sovereignty over the entire British Empire was absolute and could not be selectively denied.
May 10Parliament passes the Tea Act. The Tea Act granted the struggling British East India Company a monopoly on the American tea trade. It allowed the company to bypass English middlemen and sell directly to the colonies, significantly undercutting the prices of both local merchants and illicit smugglers. While designed to save the East India Company from bankruptcy by legally lowering tea prices, the act retained the controversial 3-pence Townshend tax on tea. The colonists viewed this as a deceptive scheme to validate Parliament’s right to tax them without their consent, triggering further widespread boycotts and protests.
December 16Boston Tea Party. The tea ship Dartmouth arrived in Boston Harbor in late November 1773, packed with chests of East India Company tea. Colonists surrounded the wharf and blocked the ship from unloading and selling the tea. On the night before the 20-day deadline for unloading expired, the Sons of Liberty boarded the Dartmouth and two other tea ships, the Eleanor and the Beaver, and dumped 342 chests of tea into the harbor in protest.
1774
March 31–June 22Parliament passes the Coercive Acts and the Quebec Act. The former punitively stripped Massachusetts of its self-governance and closed Boston Harbor to force repayment for the destroyed tea. Meanwhile, the latter further angered the mostly Protestant colonies by granting civil protections to Catholicism and expanding Canadian borders into American western territory.
September 5–October 26First Continental Congress meets in Philadelphia. Fifty-six delegates from 12 of the 13 colonies meet at Carpenters’ Hall. Convening in direct response to the Coercive Acts (also known as the Intolerable Acts), this meeting marks the first time the colonies legally unite to coordinate resistance against British rule. Delegates were deeply divided on a course of action — some wanted to compromise and find a way to patch up relations with the Crown, others wanted an aggressive, firm defense of colonial rights.
1775
April 18Paul Revere’s ride. British General Thomas Gage ordered 700 troops to secretly march to Concord to destroy a colonial weapons depot and arrest resistance leaders Samuel Adams and John Hancock, who were hiding in nearby Lexington. The plan was foiled by highly sophisticated spy networks inside Boston — Dr. Joseph Warren received definitive intelligence this evening, summoned Paul Revere and William Dawes to his home and ordered them to escape the city and spread the word.
April 19Battles of Lexington and Concord.
May 10American forces capture Fort Ticonderoga on Lake Champlain while the Second Continental Congress convenes.
June 15George Washington is appointed commander of the Continental Army.
June 17Battle of Bunker Hill.
August 23King George III declares the colonies in open rebellion.
December 31Colonists are defeated at Quebec.
1776
January 10Thomas Paine publishes Common Sense.
March 17British troops evacuate Boston.
July 4Continental Congress approves the Declaration of Independence.
August 27Battle of Long Island, New York; British take New York City.
December 25–26Washington crosses the Delaware River; Battle of Trenton.
1777
January 3Battle of Princeton, New Jersey.
September 11Battle of Brandywine, Pennsylvania.
October 4Washington is defeated at Germantown, Pennsylvania; his army retires to Valley Forge, Pennsylvania, for the winter.
October 17British general John Burgoyne surrenders at Saratoga.
November 15Articles of Confederation are approved by Congress and sent to the states for ratification.
1778
February 6France and the United States form an alliance.
1780
May 12British capture Charleston, South Carolina.
September 25Benedict Arnold flees to the British after spying for them for more than a year.
October 7British general Charles Cornwallis’s troops are forced to retreat from North Carolina.
1781
January 17Battle of Cowpens, South Carolina.
March 1Articles of Confederation are ratified.
March 15Battle of Guilford Courthouse, North Carolina.
October 19Cornwallis surrenders to Washington at Yorktown, Virginia.
1783
September 3Treaty of Peace between the Americans and British is signed.
1786
AugustShays’ Rebellion in western Massachusetts.
September 11Annapolis Convention.
1787
May 25Constitutional Convention opens in Philadelphia.
July 13Northwest Ordinance is enacted by Congress.
September 17Constitutional Convention approves the newly drafted Constitution and sends it to Congress.
October 27First of Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay’s Federalist Papers appears.
1788
January–AugustRatification of the U.S. Constitution by all states except Rhode Island and North Carolina (Delaware, Pennsylvania, and New Jersey had already ratified in December 1787).
1789
March 4First U.S. Congress under the Constitution convenes in New York.
April 30Washington is inaugurated as the first president of the United States.
1791
December 15First ten amendments to the Constitution (the Bill of Rights) are adopted.
